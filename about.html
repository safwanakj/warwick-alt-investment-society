About us

Finance has more doors than you think.

Warwick Alternative Investment Society exists to shine a light on the areas of finance that traditional finance societies don't usually cover.

Our aim is simple: raise awareness of the sectors that not everyone knows about, and build real routes into them. We do that through the people we bring in, the connections we help you make, and the firms we take you to see.

What are alternative investments?

Most students meet finance through the traditional route: shares (equities), bonds and cash, and the investment banks and asset managers built around them. An alternative investment is anything that sits outside those three. That usually means assets that are not bought and sold on a public stock exchange every day, and strategies that do something different from simply buying and holding shares.

Alternatives matter because of how big and how fast-growing they are. Pension funds, university endowments, sovereign wealth funds and family offices put large shares of their money into them, usually to diversify away from the ups and downs of public markets and to chase returns that traditional assets can't offer. They also employ a lot of people, in roles most graduates never hear about until they are already applying elsewhere.

The trade-off is real, and we'll be honest about it: alternatives are often harder to value, harder to sell quickly, more complex, and sometimes more expensive. Understanding both sides is exactly what makes them interesting to study.

The asset classes

"Alternative assets" is an umbrella term. Here are the main families we'll be exploring, from the well-established to the emerging.

Private markets

Private equity

Buying and improving companies that aren't listed on a stock exchange, from buyouts of established businesses to growth investments, then selling them years later.

Private markets

Venture capital

Backing early-stage start-ups before they have proven themselves. High risk, with the chance of outsized returns if a few bets pay off.

Private markets

Private credit

Lending directly to companies outside the traditional banking system. A fast-growing market that has taken on a big part of the lending banks once did.

Strategies

Hedge funds

Funds that use flexible strategies, such as long/short, global macro, event-driven and quantitative trading, to try to make returns in rising and falling markets.

Real assets

Real estate

Property in all its forms: offices, housing, logistics warehouses, data centres and more, held directly or through funds, for rental income and growth.

Real assets

Infrastructure

Long-life assets society depends on, like energy networks, renewables, toll roads, ports and telecoms, which tend to provide steady, long-term cash flows.

Real assets

Commodities

Raw materials such as oil, gas, metals and agricultural products, traded to gain exposure to the real economy or to protect against inflation.

Emerging

Digital assets

Cryptocurrencies and blockchain-based assets: a young, volatile and fast-changing market that institutions are still working out how to approach.

Collectibles

Art, wine and more

Passion assets such as fine art, wine, classic cars and watches. Valuing and trading them is a specialist skill of its own.

Beyond these sit even more specialist corners, including insurance-linked securities, carbon and environmental markets, royalties, litigation finance and secondaries. We'll shine a light on those too.

What we'll cover

We want every event to leave you knowing something you didn't before, and knowing someone you didn't before. Our programme will be built around:

  • How each asset class works. How money is raised, where it is put to work, how managers are paid, and how returns are made.
  • Speaker talks and panels. Investors, fund managers, founders and advisers from across alternatives, talking openly about their work, their deals and their career paths.
  • Educational sessions. Beginner-friendly workshops on topics like valuation, fund structures, deal analysis and how to read a pitch, with no prior knowledge needed.
  • Case studies and investment pitches. Working through real deals and market stories, and pitching your own ideas to your peers.
  • Careers and recruitment. What the roles actually involve, which firms hire, when applications open, and how to stand out. We'll cover the routes into the areas of finance that rarely get a mention on a standard careers fair.
  • Networking and insight days. Time with professionals and alumni, plus visits to firms to see the work first-hand.

Whether you study finance, economics, maths, law, engineering or something completely different, and whether you're a first year curious about the industry or a finalist with an offer to chase, there's a place for you.

Glossary

The jargon, decoded.

Finance loves its acronyms. Here are the terms you'll hear most at our events, in plain English.

The basics How funds work Deals and valuation Hedge funds and trading Credit, real assets and beyond

The basics

Alpha
The part of a return that comes from skill, rather than from simply being exposed to the market.
Asset class
A group of investments that behave in similar ways, such as shares, bonds or real estate.
AUM (assets under management)
The total value of the money a firm invests on behalf of its clients.
Beta
How much an investment tends to move with the wider market. A beta of 1 means it moves in line with it.
Diversification
Spreading money across different investments so that one bad outcome does less damage.
Family office
A private firm that manages the wealth of one very wealthy family, or a small group of them.
Liquidity
How quickly you can turn an investment into cash without pushing the price down.
Sovereign wealth fund
A state-owned investment fund, often built from national reserves or natural resource revenues.

How funds work

Capital call
When a fund asks its investors to hand over part of the money they promised, usually as deals come up.
Carried interest (carry)
The fund manager's share of the profits, traditionally around 20%, usually paid only after investors have been repaid.
Dry powder
Money that investors have committed to funds but that has not yet been invested.
GP (general partner)
The firm that runs a fund: it raises the money, picks the investments and manages them.
Hurdle rate
The minimum return investors must receive before the manager earns any carry, often around 8% a year.
J-curve
The typical shape of private fund returns: negative at first because of fees, then rising as investments mature and are sold.
LP (limited partner)
An investor in a fund, such as a pension fund or endowment. LPs provide most of the money but don't run the fund.
Management fee
The annual fee paid to the manager for running a fund, often 1.5% to 2% of the money involved.
Two and twenty
The classic fee model: a 2% annual management fee plus 20% of the profits.
Vintage year
The year a fund starts investing, used to compare it fairly with funds of the same age.

Deals and valuation

Cap table
A record of who owns what share of a company, and how that changes as new investors come in.
Dilution
The shrinking of existing owners' percentage stakes when a company issues new shares.
Due diligence
The detailed investigation of a business, its finances and its risks before an investment is made.
EBITDA
Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. A common, rough measure of a company's operating profit.
Enterprise value (EV)
The value of a whole business: the value of its shares, plus its debt, minus its cash.
Exit
How an investor sells up and realises a return, such as a sale to another buyer or a stock market listing (IPO).
IRR (internal rate of return)
An annualised return that takes account of when money went in and came out, so earlier gains count for more.
LBO (leveraged buyout)
Buying a company using a large amount of borrowed money, with the debt secured against the company itself.
MOIC (multiple on invested capital)
Total value returned divided by the amount invested. Turning £1m into £3m is a 3x MOIC.
Pre-money and post-money
A start-up's valuation immediately before and immediately after new investment goes in.
Secondaries
Buying existing stakes in private funds or companies from current holders, rather than investing from the start.
Series A, B, C
Successive funding rounds for a growing start-up, usually following an earlier seed round.

Hedge funds and trading

Drawdown
The fall in an investment's value from a peak to a later low, often used as a measure of risk.
Global macro
A strategy of betting on big economic trends through currencies, interest rates, bonds and commodities.
Hedging
Taking a position that offsets a risk, much like taking out insurance on another investment.
High-water mark
A fund's previous peak value. The manager usually can't charge performance fees again until it has been beaten.
Leverage
Using borrowed money to increase the size of an investment. It magnifies both gains and losses.
Long/short
Buying investments you expect to rise (long) while betting against those you expect to fall (short).
Sharpe ratio
Return above the risk-free rate, divided by volatility. It shows how much return you got for the risk you took.
Short selling
Selling a borrowed investment in the hope of buying it back more cheaply and pocketing the difference.

Credit, real assets and beyond

Cap rate
A property's annual net operating income divided by its value. A quick way of comparing real estate yields.
Catastrophe bond
An insurance-linked bond paying a high return, where investors can lose their money if a specified disaster strikes.
Covenant
A condition in a loan agreement, such as a limit on the borrower's debt, that protects the lender.
Direct lending
Non-bank lenders making loans straight to companies, the largest part of private credit.
Futures contract
An agreement to buy or sell something at a set price on a set future date, widely used in commodities.
Mezzanine debt
Lending that ranks below senior loans but above shareholders, so it carries more risk and pays more.
Stablecoin
A cryptocurrency designed to hold a steady value, usually by tracking a currency such as the US dollar.
Tokenisation
Representing ownership of an asset, such as a fund stake or property, as a digital token on a blockchain.
